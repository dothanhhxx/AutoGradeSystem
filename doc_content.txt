=== PARAGRAPHS ===
[0] Deconstructing ASAG: Combining Deterministic NLP Tags with LLM Synthesis for Interpretable Grading
[3] Abstract: Current Automated Short Answer Grading (ASAG) systems often achieve strong performance but provide limited criterion-level evidence for their grading decisions. This paper proposes HybridASAG, an interpretable framework that separates deterministic score computation from LLM-based feedback generation. Five NLP modules independently assess semantic similarity, keyword coverage, grammatical correctness, writing formality, and logical consistency. Their outputs are combined through transparent weighted aggregation, with weights optimized only on training data. Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct then generates evidence-grounded teacher-style feedback without modifying the computed score. Experiments on SemEval-2013 Task 7 and Mohler achieve 85.50% QWK, 83.50% Accuracy, and 99.12% JSON compliance.
[4] Keywords: Automated Short Answer Assessment; Deterministic Linguistic Indicators; LLMs; Interpretable Grading; Automated Feedback Generation.
[5] INTRODUCTION
[6] Automated Short Answer Grading (ASAG) enables scalable assessment while reducing instructors' grading workload [1], [2]. However, many existing systems primarily optimize scoring accuracy and provide limited criterion-level evidence explaining their decisions. Conventional end-to-end models encode reference and student answers into latent representations before predicting a score, making the grading process difficult to interpret and audit [3]. This limitation can hinder the adoption of AI-assisted assessment, particularly in formative learning [1], [2], [4].
[8] Figure 1. Conventional End-to-End ASAG Pipeline.
[9] Recent LLM-based graders can generate fluent, context-aware feedback, but their scoring decisions may be non-deterministic and difficult to trace to specific assessment criteria [1], [5], [6]. We therefore propose HybridASAG, which separates deterministic grading from LLM-based feedback generation. Five NLP modules independently assess semantic similarity, keyword coverage, grammatical correctness, writing formality, and logical consistency. Their outputs are combined through transparent weighted aggregation to determine the final grade, while Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct generates evidence-grounded teacher-style feedback without modifying the score.
[11] The main contributions are: (1) a five-dimensional deterministic NLP framework for criterion-level assessment; (2) a transparent grading mechanism that separates scoring from LLM feedback generation; and (3) an empirical evaluation on SemEval-2013 Task 7 and Mohler, including an ablation study of the proposed components. The framework is primarily evaluated on science and computer-science short answers.
[12] RELATED WORK
[13] Deep-Learning-Based ASAG
[15] ASAG has evolved from RNNs and LSTMs to transformer-based models [7], [8]. Transfer learning, data augmentation, and attention mechanisms have further improved grading performance [9]–[11], while lightweight representation-based approaches have also been explored [12]. Despite strong performance, these models generally provide holistic scores with limited criterion-level interpretability.
[16] Explainable and LLM-Based ASAG
[18] Explainable ASAG methods use feature attribution, neural additive models, neuro-symbolic reasoning, and explicit evidence extraction to improve transparency [2]–[4]. However, their explanations are often post-hoc and do not directly represent explicit linguistic criteria.
[19] LLM-based systems employ retrieval augmentation, adaptive grading, rubrics, and structured multi-agent or committee-based assessment [1], [5], [6], [14]–[17]. These approaches offer flexible and fluent feedback but may suffer from non-deterministic scoring and weak traceability.
[20] C. Positioning
[21] HybridASAG addresses this gap by separating deterministic criterion-level score computation from LLM feedback synthesis. The final grade is derived exclusively from explicit linguistic indicators, while the LLM is restricted to generating evidence-grounded feedback.

This strict separation guarantees that every numerical decision remains fully reproducible and interpretable, while the language model focuses solely on producing pedagogically useful natural-language explanations.
[79] Experiments And Results
[80] Datasets and Base Models
[81] Experiments were conducted on two established ASAG benchmarks: SemEval-2013 Task 7 [23] and the Mohler dataset. SemEval-2013 contains the Beetle and SciEntsBank subsets. Beetle contains approximately 4,000 student responses covering basic electricity and electronics, while SciEntsBank contains more than 10,000 responses covering broader science topics. Each instance includes a question, reference answer(s), student response, and human-assigned label.
[82] The Mohler dataset contains 2,442 student answers to 87 questions from an introductory computer-science course on data structures. Human scores range from 0 to 5 and are averaged from two graders. All scores are normalized to ([0,10]) for compatibility with HybridASAG.
[83] Evaluation Metrics
[84] Grading performance is evaluated using Accuracy, Macro-F1, and Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) [20]. Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified score categories, while Macro-F1 provides balanced performance across categories. QWK measures agreement with human grading while accounting for the magnitude of scoring disagreements.
[85] Two additional indicators evaluate output quality. JSON Compliance Rate measures whether generated outputs follow the required structural format and is not considered a grading-quality metric. Hallucination Rate measures the proportion of responses whose generated feedback contains at least one unsupported factual claim.
[86] For hallucination detection, an NLI-based filter first identifies potentially unsupported feedback sentences using an entailment threshold of 0.30. All flagged cases are then manually reviewed by the first author. A sample is counted as hallucinated when at least one unsupported claim is confirmed. Because only one annotator was used, this metric is considered preliminary.
[87] Experimental Setup
[88] All experiments were executed on a local NVIDIA-GPU workstation with CUDA acceleration. The five lightweight expert models were run in parallel via thread pooling, while Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct was quantized to 4-bit NF4 to reduce memory footprint. [102] Overall Performance and Ablation
[103] The Hybrid Model (Full 5+1) model shows the highest compliance of generated feedback with the JSON format (99.12%), lowest hallucination rate (1.24%), and human agreement (QWK = 85.50%) when compared to other examined models, thus proving that separation of deterministic score calculation from LLM-generated feedback production increases grading consistency and quality of feedback. In turn, an ablation analysis demonstrates the importance of using the logic module by showing how its omission decreases QWK from 85.50% to 75.20% and Accuracy and Macro F1 scores from 83.50% to 78.10% and from 82.90% to 77.54%, respectively.
[104] Latency and Error Analysis
[105] Whereas DLSAA makes some deterministic NLP calculations prior to LLM inference to produce feedback, the lightweight experts perform in parallel, but the computational burden lies in LLM inference. Future research in this area needs to focus on quantization, distillation, and other methods that can decrease the inference time needed for real-time application in the classroom. As can be seen from qualitative examples, one of the disadvantages of an LLM-based system is that it could give a high rating to incorrect answers, whereas DLSAA detects the errors at the criterion level.
[106] Conclusion and Future Work
[107] Current automated short-answer grading systems frequently prioritize scoring accuracy at the expense of transparency and useful feedback. To address this issue, we propose HybridASAG, a hybrid framework that combines five deterministic linguistic indicators for response scoring with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct for generating teacher-style feedback. The final score is obtained through a transparent weighted aggregation process, while the language model is used only to synthesize evidence-based explanations. This separation improves criterion-level interpretability and limits the risk of unsupported feedback. On the evaluated ASAG benchmarks, HybridASAG achieved a QWK of 85.5%, a JSON compliance rate of 99.12%, and a hallucination rate of 1.24%, demonstrating its potential as a reliable and interpretable grading approach. Future work will evaluate the pedagogical value of the generated feedback through human educator assessment. Further research will also examine multiple reference answers, adaptive criterion weighting, threshold calibration, inference efficiency, cross-domain generalization, and robustness against error propagation across different pipeline components.
